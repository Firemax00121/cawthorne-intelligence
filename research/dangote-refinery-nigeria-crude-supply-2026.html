Dangote Refinery Crude Supply Economics: Nigerian Feedstock, Brent Pricing and Import Flexibility

A factual research note on the crude-supply economics facing the Dangote refinery and what they reveal about Nigerian physical crude markets.

Scope: This page summarizes public market reporting and does not estimate Dangote margins, unpublished commercial terms, or proprietary refinery economics.

Why crude supply has become the central question

Reuters reported on August 26, 2026 that the Dangote refinery is preparing for a potential October IPO while investors focus on whether the refinery can secure enough crude at competitive cost as it plans to expand capacity. The refinery reached its initial 650,000 barrels-per-day design capacity and has tested production at 700,000 barrels per day, according to Reuters.

The issue is not simply national production. Nigeria is Africa's largest crude producer, but a meaningful portion of NNPC-linked crude is committed under oil-backed loans and pre-export arrangements. That reduces the pool of barrels readily available to domestic refiners.

Imports remain economically important

Dangote refinery chief executive David Bird told Reuters that imported crude accounts for roughly 30% to 40% of intake. The refinery has sourced barrels from the United States, Guyana and other African producers, giving it flexibility when domestic grades are unavailable or uneconomic.

That flexibility matters because refinery economics depend on delivered feedstock value, not nationality alone. A domestic Nigerian barrel can still be unattractive if its benchmark-linked price and commercial terms exceed the delivered value of a comparable imported grade.

Brent linkage matters to domestic pricing

Reuters reported that Dangote has argued some Nigerian crude remains expensive because NNPC prices domestic barrels against international benchmarks such as Brent while incorporating freight and logistics assumptions that a domestic refiner may not incur in the same way as an overseas buyer.

This illustrates why benchmark design matters. Nigerian crude pricing is ultimately a combination of the Brent complex, grade quality, loading terms, freight, timing, market liquidity and buyer competition. A useful physical-market reference must separate those components rather than treating an outright dollar price as sufficient.

What this means for Nigerian grades

  • Domestic demand can deepen price discovery. A large Nigerian refinery creates another credible outlet for local grades alongside export buyers.
  • Quality and yield still decide value. Light-sweet Nigerian crude competes with imported grades on refinery yield, product value and delivered economics.
  • Transparent differentials matter. Repeatable bids, offers and transactions against Dated Brent or another recognized reference would improve market transparency.
  • Logistics are part of benchmark quality. FOB terms, loading windows, cargo sizes and actual freight economics must be distinguishable from the underlying grade value.

Where Cawthorne fits

Cawthorne is an emerging Nigerian light-sweet grade with a publicly reported NNPC tender for a 950,000-barrel cargo loading September 21–22, 2026 on an FOB basis. Its relevance to a refinery such as Dangote cannot be assumed from location alone; commercial fit would depend on quality, yield, price differential, availability and competing export value.

For Cawthorne Intelligence, the broader lesson is that Nigerian crude-market infrastructure should track both export-market evidence and domestic refinery demand. Over time, repeated cargoes, transparent differentials and observable buyer behavior can help distinguish a genuinely liquid physical grade from a one-off supply event.

Signals worth watching

  • Whether Nigeria introduces a crude-swap framework matching local producers with domestic refiners.
  • How the share of imported crude at Dangote changes as capacity expands.
  • Whether domestic Nigerian grade pricing becomes more transparent on a delivered-versus-FOB basis.
  • Whether emerging grades such as Cawthorne develop repeat domestic or export buyers.
  • How Nigerian grade differentials behave relative to Brent and imported alternatives such as WTI Midland.
